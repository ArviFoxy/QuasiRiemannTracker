1 · The question

Where can an L-function vanish?

A Dirichlet character χ modulo q is a function on the integers that repeats with period q and is multiplicative: χ(mn) = χ(m)χ(n). On numbers coprime to q its values are roots of unity; on the others it is 0. Its L-function is L(s, χ) = Σn≥1 χ(n) n−s, extended to the whole complex plane.

These functions govern how the primes split among residue classes mod q. Each zero ρ of L(s, χ) adds an oscillating error of size about xRe ρ to the count of primes up to x in a class. The further right the zeros, the larger the error.

All the interesting zeros lie in the critical strip 0 < Re s < 1. The generalized Riemann hypothesis says they all lie on the line Re s = ½, which is far out of reach. This proof gives a weaker statement that holds for every character of every modulus at once:

L(s, χ) ≠ 0 whenever Re s > θ, θ = 0.87387234

The only exception is the pole of the trivial character at s = 1. A smaller θ is a stronger theorem, and the value is tracked on a public leaderboard of proofs checked by Lean.

2 · The plan

Proof by contradiction, with a detector

Suppose some L(s, χ) had a zero ρ with Re ρ > θ. The proof builds a detector, a large combination of filters tuned to χ, and computes one quantity from it in two different ways.

The first way goes through the zero. In a contour integral the zero becomes a pole of 1/L, and its residue makes the quantity large, more so the further right the zero sits. The second way uses energy bounds: proven inequalities saying that sums of many oscillating terms mostly cancel. They make the same quantity small. Past θ the two answers contradict each other, so the zero cannot exist.

In signal-processing language, L(s, χ) is the transfer function of a causal system whose impulse response is χ(n), with log-time u = log n. The inverse 1/L has impulse response μ(n)χ(n), where μ is the Möbius function. A zero with Re ρ > θ is a pole of the inverse: past θ the inverse filter would be unstable, and its response would grow like xRe ρ. The energy bounds say that growth cannot happen.

3 · Why ℤ[ω]

Moving the problem to a hexagonal lattice

The proof does not work with χ on the ordinary integers. It moves it to the Eisenstein integers ℤ[ω] = {a + bω}, with ω = e2πi/3. They form the hexagonal lattice in the complex plane, and N(a + bω) = a² − ab + b² = |a + bω|² is their norm. The lift is η(z) = χ(N(z)).

The lifted L-function factors: L(s, η) = L(s, χ)·L(s, χχ₋₃), where χ₋₃ is the character of ℚ(√−3). So a zero of L(s, χ) is also a zero of L(s, η), and it is enough to rule out zeros of L-functions on ℤ[ω].

Why this lattice? It contains the six sixth roots of unity ±1, ±ω, ±ω². That makes sixth-power residue symbols possible (section 5), and the whole filter bank is built from them.

4 · Tori

The lattice and its finite tori

Two facts about the lattice drive everything. First, multiplying by a lattice point s is a similarity of the plane: it rotates by arg s and scales by √N(s). So sℤ[ω] is a rotated, coarser copy of the same lattice.

Second, folding the lattice into one cell of sℤ[ω] gives the quotient ℤ[ω]/(s). It is a finite torus with exactly N(s) points. These tori are the signal spaces of the proof; every DFT below runs over one.

A prime of ℤ[ω] is a lattice point that does not factor further. The figure uses primes π whose norm is an ordinary prime p ≡ 1 (mod 6).

5 · Waves

Two kinds of waves

Signals on a torus come in two natural families. Write e(t) = e2πit and Tr(z) = z + z̄ = 2 Re z. The additive characters ψh(x) = e(Tr(hx / π√−3)) are plane waves, one for each frequency h. The frequencies live on the same lattice, read modulo π.

The sextic residue symbol χπ(x) = (x/π)₆ is a multiplicative character: χ(xy) = χ(x)χ(y). It is the sixth root of unity congruent to x(p−1)/6 modulo π, and 0 at x ≡ 0. It is not a wave. On the torus it looks like a spread-spectrum code.

6 · Gauss sums

Gauss sums are DFT coefficients

The Gauss sum g(π, h) = Σx χπ(x) ψh(x) is the inner product of the code with a plane wave: the DFT of χπ at frequency h. Each term is a unit phasor, so g is the end point of a walk of p − 1 unit steps. For every frequency off the period lattice the walk ends at distance exactly √p.

That is cancellation: p − 1 steps, but only √p of net progress. A detector reacting to a zero would need its phases lined up far better than this, and the energy bounds of section 7 say that rarely happens. The second button shows the fully aligned walk for comparison.

Doing this for every frequency gives the spectrum of the code. It is flat, it vanishes exactly on the period lattice, and its phase pattern is the code again, conjugated: g(π, h) = χ̄π(h)·g(π, 1). The reason is that a multiplicative character is an eigenfunction of dilation x ↦ ax, and the DFT turns dilation by a into dilation by a⁻¹.

7 · Energy bounds

Many atoms at once: frame bounds

The detector does not use one Gauss sum but very many. Each modulus u gives an atom 𝔤(u, ·) = g(u, ·)/√N(u), and the detector combines them with coefficients bu into a signal S(h) = Σu bu 𝔤(u, h).

An energy bound says Σh |S(h)|² over a window of frequencies is at most a constant times Σu |bu|². If the atoms were orthogonal, the constant would be the window size: the energy of a sum would be the sum of the energies. In signal processing this is an upper frame bound, or Bessel bound. Number theorists call it a large-sieve inequality.

How good the bound can be depends on coherence, i.e. how much atoms with different moduli correlate on the window. The product of the atoms for u and u′ repeats with period lattice uu′ℤ[ω], whose cell holds about Q² points. Once the window holds many such cells, the atoms are nearly orthogonal.

8 · Faces

What a face is

The energy bounds in the proof hold only while the detector is not too long for the family it is averaged over. The detector has two kinds of parts: plain polynomials, of total length P, and amplifier polynomials, sums over primes, of total length s. Lengths are exponents, measured relative to the family size UM. A face G(B, Λ, cap) is one proven bound of this kind: the energy is at most the diagonal value UM+ε whenever

P + Λ·s ≤ B·M, s ≤ cap·M

Divide by M. In the plane of amplifier length s and plain length P, each face is the region under a line, with height B at s = 0 and slope −Λ. Amplifier parts are charged Λ times their nominal length; a smaller Λ means the amplifier behaves more like its nominal bandwidth. Anything under at least one face is proven, so the proven region is everything under the upper envelope of the lines.

9 · One step

How a step earns a better face

Each step of the chain proves a new face from the previous one. The new line is a little flatter (Λ drops by 1/200) and starts a little lower (B drops slightly), so it is higher than its parent beyond the crossing point s₀ = (B₀ − B)/(Λ₀ − Λ). That sliver is the step's new territory, and the step must pay for it. The sliver is too thin to see at full scale, so here it is under a magnifier.

The payment comes from an exact identity that moves one amplifier prime out of the problem. For a modulus s = D·n with coprime parts, the Chinese remainder theorem splits the torus: ℤ[ω]/(Dn) ≅ ℤ[ω]/(D) × ℤ[ω]/(n). Under the index map x = a·n + b·D the plane waves factor, and the code factors up to one constant phase. So the DFT factors with no twiddle factors, as in the prime-factor (Good–Thomas) FFT.

The constant phase is χD(n)·χn(D). Sextic reciprocity gives χD(n) = ±χn(D). The leftover χn(D)² becomes a dilation of the frequency by D⁴, because χ⁶ = 1 makes D⁻² act like D⁴. The result is the transfer identity:

g(Dn, h) = ± g(D, h) · g(n, D⁴h)

The prime D has left the modulus and come back as a dilation of the frequency. Geometrically, h ↦ D⁴h rotates by 4·arg D and magnifies by N(D)², which is 49 for N(D) = 7. In the proof D runs over a whole slot of primes, so the parent's frequency window is spread over a window N(D)⁴ times larger in area.

The step's accounting has three entries, each measured per unit of the selected primes' length d:

  • Room e = 1. The parent family has about Ud times more members than the residual. Averaged per member, the residual's bound comes with a saving U−d.
  • Removed width r = 1. The selected prime is no longer part of the amplifier, so its length no longer costs Λ.
  • Scale cost c = 6. The previous face is applied to a problem that is smaller by U6d: 4 from the dilated window and 2 from the smaller moduli.

Before applying the face, the step sorts the residual's rows. LOW rows are those where the amplifier fires weakly; they are deleted cheaply and handled by the energy bound. HIGH rows are those where it fires strongly; there are few of them, and they are counted with zero-density estimates. The threshold between the two is the density cut q. The step is affordable while e + q·r − c·q·B/Λ ≥ 0.

10 · The wall

Why the chain stops

A higher cut q means fewer HIGH rows to count, so each step wants q as large as possible. The affordability condition caps it: q ≤ 0.999·Λ/(6B − Λ). In the (Λ, B) plane every face is a point and every value of q is a line through the origin, B = Λ·(1 + 0.999/q)/6. A face can still take a step at cut q only while it lies below that line.

The chain starts on the q = ¾ line and walks left until it reaches the q = ½ line, the lowest cut the HIGH counting accepts. There it stops.

The way through the wall is a better identity. With constants (e, c, r) the lines become B = Λ·(r + e/q)/c, ignoring the 0.999. More room e or a smaller cost c makes them steeper, so the same floor reaches further left. A proven theorem shows that identities of this type without a leftover twist cannot have c below 6. That leaves twisted identities, and more room.

More room could come from squared primes. If the modulus is D², the code χD² = χD² depends only on x mod D, a shorter period than its torus. Its spectrum is therefore a comb: zero except at frequencies divisible by D exactly once, where the normalized atom is √N(D) tall instead of 1. That is the same energy concentrated on 1/N(D) of the frequencies, and that sparsity is worth extra room.

In a back-of-envelope accounting that is not yet proven, a squared block has e = 2, which would let the chain run on to Λ ≈ 1.2·B. A cost c = 3 would allow Λ ≈ B. Neither transfer is available yet: squared primes need a source theorem that supplies them, and no cubic identity of this kind is known.

11 · The return

How θ comes out

The chain's last faces feed the comparison of section 2. The final comparison is not checked in one piece. Its parameter range is split into 31 windows, and each window is handled by one of six counting methods with its own certificate. θ is the largest real part at which every window still wins.

Everything before this point is unconditional: the faces are theorems about energy, true whether or not zeros exist. The zero enters only here, as the residue that the face-based bound cannot accommodate.

Glossary

Terms used on this page

Dirichlet character χ mod q
A q-periodic multiplicative function on the integers, with roots of unity as values on numbers coprime to q.
L-function, zero, θ
L(s, χ) = Σ χ(n)n−s, extended to ℂ. A zero is an s with L(s, χ) = 0; θ is a proven bound on the real parts of all zeros.
ℤ[ω], norm
The hexagonal lattice a + bω with ω = e2πi/3; N(a + bω) = a² − ab + b².
torus ℤ[ω]/(s)
The lattice folded into one cell of the coarser lattice sℤ[ω]; a finite group with N(s) points.
plane wave ψh
An additive character on the torus, one for each frequency h.
sextic code χπ
The sixth-power residue symbol (x/π)₆, a multiplicative character with six values.
Gauss sum g(π, h)
The DFT of the code at frequency h; |g| = √p off the period lattice.
atom, frame bound
An atom is a normalized Gauss sum, one per modulus. A frame (Bessel) bound limits the energy of any combination of atoms; in number theory it is called a large-sieve inequality.
face G(B, Λ, cap)
A proven energy bound, valid while P + Λ·s ≤ B·M and s ≤ cap·M.
step, crossing s₀
A step proves a flatter face from the previous one; the new line beats its parent beyond s₀.
transfer
The identity g(Dn, h) = ±g(D, h)·g(n, D⁴h), which moves a prime from the modulus into the frequency.
room e, width r, cost c
What a transfer saves per unit of selected length, what it removes from the amplifier, and how much scale it costs.
density cut q, LOW and HIGH rows
Rows where the amplifier fires weakly (LOW) are handled by energy; rows where it fires strongly (HIGH) are counted. q is the threshold between them.
return
The final stage, which turns the last faces into θ.
